# Phishing Red Flags: A Quick Guide

Phishing is a social-engineering attack in which a criminal pretends to be a trusted person or organisation to trick you into clicking a malicious link, opening an infected attachment, or handing over sensitive information such as passwords, one-time codes or banking details.

## Common types

| Type | Channel | Typical example |
|------|---------|-----------------|
| Email phishing | Email | "Your mailbox is full. Verify your account now." |
| Spear phishing | Email | A message that uses your name, course or job title to look genuine |
| Smishing | SMS / messaging apps | "Your parcel is held. Pay the delivery fee here." |
| Vishing | Phone call | A caller posing as bank or IT support who asks for your OTP |
| Clone phishing | Email | A copy of a real email with the link or attachment swapped for a malicious one |

## Eight red flags

1. **Urgency or threats.** "Act within 24 hours or your account will be closed." Attackers want you to act before you think.
2. **Mismatched sender.** The display name says "Student Support" but the address is `support@stud3nt-help.example`. Always check the actual address, not just the name.
3. **Suspicious links.** The visible text says one thing; the real destination (shown when you hover) says another. Watch for look-alike spellings, extra hyphens, many subdomains, IP addresses, `@` symbols and URL shorteners.
4. **Requests for secrets.** Genuine organisations never ask for your password, PIN or one-time code by email, SMS or phone.
5. **Unexpected attachments.** Invoices, "voice messages" or "scanned documents" you were not expecting, especially `.zip`, `.html`, `.iso` and macro-enabled Office files.
6. **Generic greetings.** "Dear customer" or "Dear user" from an organisation that normally uses your name.
7. **Poor language or formatting.** Odd grammar, low-quality logos or inconsistent fonts. (Do not rely on this alone: many modern phishing emails are well written.)
8. **Too good to be true.** Prizes, refunds or job offers you did not apply for.

## What to do when you spot one

1. **Stop.** Do not click, reply, scan the QR code or open the attachment.
2. **Verify.** Contact the organisation using a phone number or website you already trust, not the one in the message.
3. **Report.** Send it to your SOC / IT helpdesk using the [incident report template](../templates/phishing-incident-report-template.md).
4. **Delete.** Remove the message once it has been reported.

If you already interacted with the message, follow the [response checklist](../checklists/phishing-response-checklist.md) immediately.

## Safe habits

- Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) for email, social media and your student portal.
- Use a password manager and a unique password for every account.
- Type the website address yourself or use a bookmark instead of following links.
- Keep your browser, operating system and security software up to date.
